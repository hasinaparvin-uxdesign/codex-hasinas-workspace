# eBitans: from website setup to everyday commerce

## Helping entrepreneurs build and manage a store with less technical dependency

**Hasina Parvin Deepa · UX/UI Designer & Design Team Lead**

I helped shape and evolve eBitans, an e-commerce website builder for Bangladeshi entrepreneurs. My work connected customer problem framing, guided store creation, design-team coordination and implementation review as the product expanded into everyday commerce operations.

**Scope:** early product development through post-launch evolution. **Collaboration:** a small design team, developers, and product and management stakeholders.

> **Evidence and provenance:** This is a retrospective account reconstructed from written recollection, supplied presentation material and a public-site audit conducted on 27 October 2026. The original work was not organized as a formal Figma file. Newly drawn interfaces are labeled “Reconstructed concept — not an original screenshot.”

## 1. The customer problem extended beyond getting a website

During earlier customer-facing work at Webbox, I met business owners who needed help creating and maintaining online storefronts. Buying a theme was only one part of the work. Adapting it, adding products and making routine changes still required technical support.

Other difficulties appeared around daily selling. Some businesses relied heavily on Facebook pages, groups or live selling. Product information, stock, payments and courier updates could sit in separate workflows. A website alone would not address all of that operational effort.

These patterns came from customer conversations, meetings and feedback during delivery. They were qualitative observations, rather than a study with a recorded sample size or statistically representative findings.

I framed the opportunity around two related needs: help a less technical entrepreneur reach a recognizable storefront, then help them keep that store useful as the business operates.

## 2. My role: carry the customer need through design and implementation

As UX/UI Designer & Design Team Lead, I led or contributed to experience decisions and took primary responsibility for complex or new design work. I divided tasks across a small design team and coordinated its direction.

I worked with developers to discuss feasibility, brought important decisions to product and management stakeholders, and reviewed implementation against the intended customer need. Customer feedback continued to inform later changes and the way features were explained.

The platform was a collective effort. My contribution was to connect problem framing, design decisions, team coordination and delivery—not to build every part of the product alone.

## 3. Decision one: focus the first flow on a useful storefront

Our initial hypothesis was that a short guided setup and a relevant theme could help a business owner understand the product through a tangible result. The first scope concentrated on website generation and customization with a small theme set before the platform expanded.

My remembered early sequence was:

**Register or log in → choose a business category → enter a store name → choose a relevant theme → create the website → review and customize.**

The rationale for category before theme was relevance. Business context could inform the theme choices presented next. Store identity then carried into the generated website. This describes our design intention; it is not evidence that the sequence was validated through a formal usability test.

The journey changed over time. My later recollection includes phone verification. An undated supplied presentation instead shows name and category together, followed by subdomain and currency setup. The accompanying Figma evidence pages preserve these versions separately. I do not treat them as one timeless flow or use the presentation's promotional click count as a measured outcome.

## 4. Decision two: recognize the limits of self-service customization

Store creation was a beginning. Customers also needed to adapt colors, typography, layouts, navigation, sections and other site elements to their businesses.

That flexibility came with a tension: some people could manage the controls, while complete beginners could still need help. Offering more choices did not automatically make the product approachable.

The support approach extended beyond the interface. We created tutorial videos, included learning content within the platform, and shared guidance through WhatsApp. Calls and office visits helped explain the product and reassure people before payment.

For the retrospective presentation, I use a controls-and-preview concept to explain customization. Its exact layout is a new explanatory reconstruction. The public learning page inspected on 27 October 2026 separately evidenced topics covering products, themes, menus, banners, pages and domains.

My reflection is that guidance belongs near the point of uncertainty. Education and human support were part of the wider experience, although their effectiveness was not measured in the evidence available for this case study.

## 5. Decision three: evolve around the work of running a business

As the product developed, customer requirements extended beyond site creation. Four examples explain the shift toward everyday operations. These are recalled feature stories, not a dated release chronology or a claim that everything launched together.

| Customer need | Product response described in my account | UX intent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sellers repeated information across order and courier workflows | Courier integration | Reduce duplicate handling and keep fulfilment work connected |
| Payment practices varied across customers and selling situations | Mobile payments, with Cash on Delivery and card payments added later | Reflect local commerce practices rather than assume a single payment model |
| Businesses wanted to connect advertising activity with sales tracking | Facebook Pixel integration | Make advertising measurement a product capability without presenting unverified conversion results |
| Some businesses sold both online and from physical shops | Inventory and POS capabilities | Support product and stock work across selling contexts |

The public services page inspected on 27 October 2026 described POS and inventory offerings, including shared product management and stock-related capabilities. That supports the public product scope at the time of the audit. It does not verify private feature workflows or prove the effect of those features on business performance.

## 6. How the team moved from a signal to a shipped feature

The process became more structured over time. A recurring customer need led to problem framing and design discussion. I coordinated the design work and collaborated with developers on feasibility. Important decisions went through stakeholder review, followed by implementation and review against the requirement.

The work continued after release: explaining the feature, listening to feedback and considering further changes. I describe this as an evolving collaborative process, rather than retrospectively imposing a formal research or delivery framework on every stage.

**Customer signal → problem framing → design and coordination → developer feasibility → stakeholder review → implementation review → education and feedback.**

## 7. Outcomes supported by the available evidence

The defensible outcome is a product that, in my historical account, expanded from guided website creation into customization and broader commerce support. The public audit provides separate evidence of registration, themes, learning resources, POS and inventory positioning in October 2026.

This case study demonstrates the scope of the product and my contribution to its experience decisions and collaborative delivery. It does not claim a measured improvement in conversion, setup time, usability or revenue. Confidential business metrics and inferred paid-customer counts are excluded.

The reconstructed interfaces make the reasoning inspectable, but they are not substitutes for historical screenshots or evidence of formal usability testing.

## 8. What I would carry forward

**Design for the next task, not only the first milestone.** A generated storefront is useful only if the owner can continue adding products, making changes and handling orders.

**Treat relevance and confidence as design concerns.** A relevant theme can make an early choice easier to understand. Tutorials and human support can address uncertainty that interface controls alone leave unresolved.

**Let local operating practices shape the product.** Payment, fulfilment and physical-store needs deserve attention alongside the storefront itself.

**Stay involved through implementation and evolution.** Design leadership includes coordinating people, discussing feasibility and checking whether the delivered experience still addresses the original need.
