# My Anti-AI-Slop Rules

## 1. AI Output I Would Never Show a Client

I would never present a design to a client if it looks generic, fails to solve the user's actual problem, ignores the product's target audience, or relies on repetitive AI-generated layouts, gradients, cards, and typography.

**My rule:** If a design looks like a generic AI-generated template rather than a thoughtful solution tailored to the product, its users, and business goals, it is not ready to show to a client.

## 2. My Three Personal Anti-Slop Rules

### Rule 1: Unnecessary Visual Noise

I reject unnecessary gradients, glowing shapes, decorative effects, meaningless icons, distracting animations, and excessive visual elements that do not contribute to the experience.

**Fix:** Use visual elements only when they communicate meaning, support the brand, guide attention, or improve the user experience.

### Rule 2: Confusing Hierarchy and Repetitive Content

I reject competing primary buttons, repetitive cards or sections, generic headlines, vague marketing copy, and unclear navigation that make it difficult for users to understand what matters or what to do next.

**Fix:** Establish a clear information hierarchy, use specific and meaningful content, and make the next action obvious at every step.

### Rule 3: Poor Real-World User Experience

I reject designs that look attractive but make it difficult for users to find information, complete tasks, navigate between steps, understand payment status, or recover from errors.

**Fix:** Design around real user needs and end-to-end tasks. Make navigation predictable, instructions clear, forms manageable, payment states understandable, and errors easy to recover from. Use readable, accessible colors that respect the brand.

## 3. Banned Words and Generic Marketing Copy

Avoid these words and phrases in product headlines, buttons, descriptions, and marketing copy when they are vague, overused, or fail to communicate specific value.

1. **Empower** — Explain what users can actually accomplish.
2. **Seamlessly** — Describe how the process works or what makes it easier.
3. **Leverage** — Use clear, everyday language to explain what users can do.
4. **Robust** — Specify the actual capabilities, reliability, or benefits of the product.
5. **Intuitive** — Show how the interface helps users understand and complete tasks without unnecessary effort.
6. **Beautifully crafted** — Explain what makes the product valuable or distinctive instead of relying on a vague aesthetic claim.

**My rule:** Product copy must communicate specific value, real capabilities, or a meaningful user benefit. Never use polished-sounding language as a substitute for clear communication.

These words are not forbidden in every conceivable context. The default is to avoid vague, unsupported marketing language and choose clearer, more specific alternatives.

## 4. My Specificity Test

A first-time customer uses an Android phone on a slow mobile connection to find a product, review the full price and delivery costs, enter delivery details, check the order summary, complete payment, and clearly understand whether the payment succeeded and the order was confirmed.

**My rule:** A design must reflect the real user's context, device, needs, and complete task. If the design would remain exactly the same regardless of the product, user, or situation, it is not specific enough.

## 5. A Rule I Deliberately Break: Avoiding Motion

I deliberately break the rule of avoiding animations when purposeful motion can improve the user experience, communicate the product's value, demonstrate how something works, or make an interaction easier to understand.

For example, on a car website, an animation showing individual vehicle parts coming together to form a complete car can communicate the product in an engaging and memorable way.

**My rule:** Use motion when it serves a clear purpose and improves the experience. Do not add animation to every section or use it merely for decoration. Keep routine transitions responsive, and ensure that complex animations do not distract users, delay essential tasks, or compromise accessibility and performance.
